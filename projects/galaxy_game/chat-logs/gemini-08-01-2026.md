Building an artificial magnetosphere for Mars is theoretically possible using current physics and near-future technology. Because restarting Mars's dead internal core dynamo is energetically impossible with modern or near-future tech, realistic proposals focus on placing electromagnetic shielding outside the planet or in orbit.

Here are the three primary engineering approaches proposed by scientists, ranked from most practical to most complex.

1. The L1 Magnetic Shield (The NASA / Jim Green Model)
Rather than encompassing the whole planet, this approach places a magnetic generator at the Sun-Mars L 
1
​
  Lagrange point (about 1 million km sunward of Mars).

Mechanism: The station generates a magnetic dipole field oriented opposite to the interplanetary magnetic field. This creates a bow shock that deflects the solar wind, casting a protective "magnetic umbrella" (magnetosheath) over the entire planet.

Magnetic Field: Requires a field strength of roughly 1 to 2 Tesla at the station.

Power Needs: Remarkably modest—estimated between 1 to 2 Megawatts (MW), which could be powered by a single compact nuclear reactor or a large solar array.

Primary Advantage: Lowest material mass requirement. By placing the shield far upstream at L

2. Orbital Plasma Torus (The Phobos / Bamford Model)
Proposed by researchers led by Ruth Bamford, this system uses Mars's inner moon, Phobos, to create a ring of charged particles around the planet.

Mechanism: Off-gas material stripped from Phobos (such as water or carbon dioxide) is ionized and injected into orbit. Orbital particle accelerators or RF transmitters on Phobos accelerate these ions, creating a massive equatorial ring current (a toroidal plasma current).

Magnetic Field: Generates a global dipole field on the order of 30–50 μT (microteslas), matching Earth's natural surface field (~30–60 μT).

Power Needs: Roughly 10 to 100 MW to continuously energize the plasma torus against particle loss into space.

Primary Advantage: Provides true global coverage without relying on a single point of failure at L 
1
​
 .

3. Planetary Superconducting Loops
This brute-force engineering method involves constructing massive superconducting cables around Mars's equator or in low orbit.

Mechanism: A surface or orbital ring carrying millions of amperes of direct current (DC) to generate a dipole magnetic field.

Scale & Mass: An equatorial ring would span roughly 21,300 km. Construction would require massive mining and manufacturing infrastructure on Mars to produce high-temperature superconductors (like YBCO) locally.

Challenges: Requires active cryogenic refrigeration across thousands of kilometers of cable, as well as high redundancy to prevent localized faults from collapsing the entire field.
