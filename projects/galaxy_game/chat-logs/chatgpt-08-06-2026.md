My rule of thumb

I would aim for roughly:

50-100 raw resources
100-200 industrial materials/components
Thousands of final blueprints

That sounds backwards, but it's exactly how real manufacturing works. A relatively small catalog of standardized parts gets reused in countless products.

I think NASA is actually a good model here. They don't design a new valve, connector, pressure vessel, or electrical bus for every spacecraft. They rely on standardized, qualified components. Your cycler, lunar habitats, Mars bases, orbital stations, and transport craft should all draw from the same library of structural beams, pressure modules, tanks, pumps, radiators, power units, and avionics. That gives players the satisfaction of building an industrial ecosystem instead of chasing ever-growing lists of one-off materials.

Well remeber the first 3d printed ibeams are rough using depleted regolith

Exactly, and I think that's an important visual distinction to preserve. The Mk1 cycler shouldn't look like it was built from modern aerospace materials—it should look like it was assembled from the first structural products Luna was capable of making.

The Mk1 depleted-regolith I-beams are almost a proof of concept:

Thick oversized flanges because the material isn't very strong.
Rough layered texture from the early large-scale printer.
Rounded edges from sintering rather than machining.
Occasional reinforcement collars where high loads are expected.
Large bolted or pinned joints because welding or high-quality bonding isn't yet practical.
Dark gray color with subtle variations from impurities in the regolith.

The same philosophy applies to the panels.

Instead of aluminum hull plating, the cycler uses printed regolith structural panels that are:

Thick (perhaps 15–30 cm depending on purpose).
Ribbed on the back for stiffness.
Non-load-bearing in many locations—they mainly provide enclosure, micrometeoroid protection, and a base for radiation shielding.
Manufactured in standard sizes so robots can easily replace damaged panels.

From a distance, the cycler would resemble a giant orbital truss made of concrete-colored members with white cylindrical Earth-built modules inserted into it.

I also like the gameplay implications. The player's first lunar industry isn't producing spacecraft—it's producing construction materials:

Depleted regolith → Mk1 I-beams
Depleted regolith → structural nodes
Depleted regolith → wall panels
Depleted regolith → deck plates
Depleted regolith → shielding blocks

Only after that foundation exists can they start assembling very large structures.

Later technology levels naturally evolve:

Mk1: Depleted-regolith printed members. Very heavy, rough, oversized.
Mk2: Metal-reinforced regolith composites with embedded steel or aluminum tension members.
Mk3: High-strength lunar alloys produced from refined regolith, allowing much lighter trusses.
Mk4: Composite lattice beams with integrated conduits, sensors, and attachment points.

Visually, a Mk1 cycler would almost tell the story of humanity's first industrial expansion into space. You'd immediately recognize it as something built from lunar rock, not from Earth-launched aerospace components. Then, as players unlock better manufacturing, newer cyclers become progressively lighter, cleaner, and more sophisticated while still retaining the same underlying modular construction system. That evolution would make the technological progression immediately visible in-game.
